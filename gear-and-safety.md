# Gear and eye safety

## Eye safety — the non-negotiable part

Looking at the partial phases without proper filters causes permanent retinal damage, and it does
not hurt while it happens. There are no pain receptors in the retina. This is the one part of the
trip with an irreversible failure mode, so it gets treated seriously across the whole group.

**Rules:**

1. **Filters on for every moment except totality.** The partial phases before and after are the
   dangerous ones. Sunglasses — any number of pairs — are not eye protection.
2. **During totality, and only during totality, filters come off.** This is the whole reason to be
   there, and it is safe *only* while the sun is 100% covered. Which means being inside the path,
   not near it: outside the path there is no safe moment at all.
3. **Filters back on the instant the first sliver returns.** Have one person call it out loud.
4. **Never look through a camera, binoculars or a telescope with eclipse glasses alone.** Optics
   concentrate the light and will burn through the filter and then through the eye. Front-mounted
   solar filters on the optics themselves are what make those safe.

**What to buy:** eclipse glasses certified to **ISO 12312-2**, from a supplier on the American
Astronomical Society's vetted list. Counterfeits carrying a fake ISO stamp are widespread, so the
vendor matters more than the printing on the glasses.

**When to buy: 2028, not now.** Filters degrade, stock sits in warehouses, and buying two years
early risks both. Buy a generous surplus — a dozen-plus for six people — because they get dropped,
scratched, sat on and lent to strangers, and a scratched filter is a discarded filter.

## Photography

Decide early whether anyone is seriously shooting, because it changes what gets carried and, more
importantly, how the four minutes are spent.

The standing advice from experienced eclipse watchers: **on a first eclipse, do not photograph it.**
Totality is short, the sky does something genuinely unearthly, and it is easy to spend all of it
looking at a screen and fumbling settings. If someone wants images, one person shoots on a locked-off
tripod with an intervalometer and the others just watch.

If shooting the partial phases: a proper **front-mounted solar filter** on the lens, not a screw-in
filter behind it. During totality the filter comes off; afterwards it goes straight back on.

## Weather and clothing

July is **Australian winter**:

- **Inland (NT / WA / western Queensland):** warm to hot days, genuinely cold nights and dawns in the
  desert. Layers, and more than people expect for "the outback."
- **Sydney and coastal NSW:** mild days, cool evenings, and rain is entirely possible.
- Sun protection regardless — the UV is strong even in winter, and eclipse day involves standing
  outside for hours.

## Kit list

Per person:

- ISO 12312-2 eclipse glasses (plus spares)
- Layers, hat, sunscreen, refillable water
- Any medication in carry-on, with a supply buffer

Group:

- Folding chairs — totality is the short part; the wait is not
- Shade, water well beyond expectation, snacks
- Offline maps downloaded in advance, and a paper backup if inland — see the mobile coverage gaps
  noted in [`eclipse-facts.md`](eclipse-facts.md)
- Power banks
- First-aid kit, more substantial if the site is hours from a hospital
